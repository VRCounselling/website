Why asking for help feels so hard

By Valessa Rizzotto, MA, RCC, CCC · February 2026

There is a particular experience I often hear about in therapy: people who spend months thinking about reaching out, researching therapists, writing and rewriting a message, and then deciding not to send it. People who tell others they are fine when they are struggling. People who carry difficult things on their own, sometimes for much longer than they need to.

Maybe you recognize yourself in this.

Asking for help sounds simple. In reality, it requires something many of us were not always taught: the belief that our needs matter, that needing support does not make us a burden, and that allowing ourselves to receive care does not mean we are weak.

For some people, these beliefs developed through early experiences where relying on others felt difficult, uncertain, or unfamiliar. Support may not always have been available, or they may have learned to become the person who managed, adapted, and handled things on their own.

Over time, the conclusion may have become that the safest option was to need as little as possible.

At one point, this way of coping may have been helpful. It may have allowed you to navigate challenges, stay independent, and keep moving forward. The difficulty is that what once protected us can sometimes become limiting later in life, making it harder to allow others to support us.

Asking for help is not simply a personality trait that some people have and others do not. It develops through relationships where it becomes possible to experience that our needs can be met and that reaching out does not have to come with shame or fear.

Therapy can become one of those relationships. For many people, the first step of reaching out is the hardest part. That step itself can be meaningful.

Exploring the difficulty of asking for help can be the beginning of understanding how these patterns developed and what new possibilities may become available.
