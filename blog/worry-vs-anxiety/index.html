Anxiety

The difference between worry and anxiety, and why it matters

By Valessa Rizzotto, MA, RCC, CCC · June 2026

Most of us use the words interchangeably. I'm so anxious about this presentation. I'm worried about my relationship. I've been anxious all week. And in everyday language, that is perfectly fine.

But in the therapy room, the distinction matters. Because worry and anxiety, while related, are different experiences that call for different kinds of attention.

Worry lives in the mind

Worry is largely a cognitive experience. It is the mind's attempt to think its way to safety, to anticipate problems, run through scenarios and prepare for what might go wrong. In moderate amounts, worry is actually useful. It motivates us to prepare, to plan, to take things seriously.

The trouble begins when the thinking never stops. When every scenario leads to another, darker one. When the preparation never feels like enough. At that point, worry has stopped serving you and started consuming you.

Anxiety lives in the body

Anxiety is more diffuse, and more physical. It is that background hum of unease that does not always attach to a specific thought. The tightness in your chest before you can name what you are afraid of. The restlessness, the irritability, the sense that something is wrong without being able to say exactly what.

Anxiety often has roots that run deeper than the present situation. It tends to carry echoes of older experiences, times when the world felt unpredictable or unsafe, when your needs were not met, when you learned that certain feelings were not welcome. It is not irrational, even when it feels that way. It is a response that once made sense, and that the body has not yet been persuaded to let go of.

How anxiety shows up in daily life

For many adults, anxiety does not look like panic. It looks like difficulty switching off. Like being the person who always anticipates what could go wrong. Like snapping at people you love and not quite knowing why. Like a persistent sense of not doing enough, not being enough, even when the evidence suggests otherwise.

It can look like over-functioning too. Staying busy, staying productive, staying useful, as a way of keeping the anxiety at bay.

When does worry become a problem?

Worry becomes worth taking seriously when it is taking up more space than the situation warrants. When it is affecting your sleep, your relationships and your ability to be present. When you find yourself avoiding things, or needing to control situations more than feels comfortable.

Anxiety becomes worth exploring when it feels chronic. A steady undercurrent rather than a response to a specific event. When it has been there, in some form, for as long as you can remember.

What therapy offers

One of the most relieving things people discover in therapy is that their anxiety is not random and it is not a character flaw. It developed for reasons. It has been doing a job, and often, that job is protecting something vulnerable underneath.

Psychodynamic therapy creates space to slow down and get curious about that job. What is the anxiety guarding? What has it learned to expect? What would it mean to feel safe?

You do not have to live with the hum always in the background.
