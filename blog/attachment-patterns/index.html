Understanding attachment patterns in relationships

By Valessa Rizzotto, MA, RCC, CCC · May 2026

Have you ever noticed that you sometimes find yourself repeating the same patterns in relationships, even when you recognize what is happening and wish you could respond differently?

You pull away when someone gets too close. You hold on tighter when someone seems to pull away. Or you find yourself feeling anxious in a way that does not quite match what is happening between you and the other person.

These patterns are often connected to attachment. They are not a reflection of a personal flaw, but ways of adapting that developed through our early experiences with others.

Attachment theory, originally developed by John Bowlby and later expanded by other researchers, begins with a simple observation: we are wired for connection from the very beginning. As children, we develop ways of staying close to the people we depend on, and those patterns are shaped by how consistently and safely our needs were met. Whether comfort was available. Whether our needs were met with warmth, inconsistency, or not at all.

The patterns we develop in those early relationships do not stay in childhood. They can influence our friendships, romantic relationships, and the ways we respond when someone we depend on disappoints us or feels unavailable. Often, these responses happen long before we fully understand what is driving them.

Someone who learned early that closeness was unpredictable may find themselves seeking reassurance or creating emotional distance as a way of protecting themselves. Someone who learned that needing others felt unsafe may become highly self-sufficient while carrying a deep sense of loneliness underneath.

None of this is fixed. But these patterns rarely shift simply through awareness or by deciding to respond differently.

Attachment patterns are not only thoughts or beliefs. They can also show up through our emotional reactions, our body responses, and the ways we move toward or away from connection before we are fully aware of what is happening.

Change often begins through a relationship where these patterns can surface, be noticed without judgment, and gradually begin to soften. A relationship where closeness can begin to feel safe.

This is often where new ways of relating to yourself and to others begin to develop.
