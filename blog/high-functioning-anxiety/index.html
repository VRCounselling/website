When anxiety looks like having it together

By Valessa Rizzotto, MA, RCC, CCC · June 2026

There is a form of anxiety that often goes unnoticed. It does not always show up as feeling overwhelmed or unable to cope. Sometimes, it appears as being highly responsible, prepared, and in control, even when maintaining that level of functioning comes with a significant internal cost.

Note: "High-functioning anxiety" is not a formal clinical diagnosis. It is a commonly used term to describe an experience in which anxiety may be hidden beneath a person's ability to manage responsibilities, perform well, and appear capable from the outside. Even when it does not look like distress, it can still be a meaningful experience worth exploring.

It may look like being the most prepared person in the room, always double-checking, arriving early, anticipating problems before they happen, and making sure things do not fall through the cracks. It may look like a full schedule, a long list of responsibilities, and a life organized carefully around staying on top of everything.

From the outside, it can look like competence and dedication. On the inside, it can feel exhausting, as though everything depends on continuing to hold things together.

This is sometimes described as high-functioning anxiety. One of the challenging parts is that these patterns are often reinforced by the people around us. You may be seen as reliable, responsible, and someone who can always be counted on. Others may not see the worry, pressure, or difficulty allowing yourself to truly rest underneath it.

A strong need for control is often connected to this experience. When our inner world feels uncertain or difficult to predict, creating order in the outside world can become a way of feeling safer.

If I can keep everything organized, anticipate what might happen, and stay prepared, maybe I can prevent things from going wrong.

And to some extent, this strategy works. It may have helped you succeed, adapt, and navigate difficult situations. But it can also come with a cost: constant mental effort, difficulty slowing down, and feeling as though you always need to stay one step ahead.

Because anxiety can be hidden beneath what looks like competence, it often goes unrecognized for a long time. Others may not think someone who appears to be functioning well needs support. And the person themselves may minimize what they are experiencing: I have no reason to feel this way. I should be grateful. Other people have it worse.

But functioning is not the same as feeling well.

The effort it takes to hold everything together, day after day, is real. It deserves more than simply finding ways to become even more efficient. It deserves curiosity, understanding, and space to explore what may be underneath.
